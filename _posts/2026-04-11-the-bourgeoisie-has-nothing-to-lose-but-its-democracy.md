---
title: "The Bourgeoisie Has Nothing to Lose but Its Democracy"
description: "Class Structure and Democratic Weakness in Post-Communist Europe"
---

*I wrote this essay for a graduate-level course on European politics at McGill.*

Recent theoretical approaches to democratic backsliding in Europe have focused on elite behaviour,[^1] institutional weakness,[^2] or mainstream party failure.[^3] Coalitional approaches based on the Ricardian economic classes, which have been used to explain the first wave of democracy,[^4] have largely been neglected in the literature on democratic backsliding in post-communist Europe.[^5] This essay applies Ansell and Samuels’s[^6] (2010) class-based theory of bourgeois democratization not to explain how post-communist Europe democratized, but to show why democracy remained fragile once it arrived. Because communism nationalized the economy, it prevented the formation of a capital-owning class independent from the state, depriving post-communist Europe of the social group with the most to lose from an unconstrained state. A traditional bourgeoisie failed to form in post-communist Europe due to weak property rights and the transfer of former state assets to foreign investors and the nomenklatura, who were able to co-opt the state rather than needing to contest it. Lacking this pro-democratic class, post-communist states were more susceptible to backsliding.

Unlike redistributivist theories,[^7] which argue that democratization comes from elites’ calculations about the costs of redistribution, in “Inequality and Democratization,” Ansell and Samuels propose a demand-side account: democratization originates in rising economic groups’ struggle to obtain protection for their wealth from the state. In the 18<sup>th</sup> and 19<sup>th</sup> centuries, a bourgeoisie rapidly enriched by industrialization found itself vulnerable to expropriation by the aristocracy, which controlled the state. Unable to co-opt political institutions directly, the bourgeoisie demanded democratic reforms as a shield for its newfound wealth.

Ansell and Samuels’s theory is supported by empirical results showing that a more powerful aristocracy (indicated by higher land inequality) hinders democratization, whereas a more powerful bourgeoisie (indicated by higher non-land inequality) supports democratization. While their theory concerns democratization, it can reasonably be applied to the issue of democratic backsliding as well if one assumes that democracy is harder to sustain without the presence of a class independent of the state which has an economic interest in democracy’s continued success; i.e., a lack of demand-side pressure for democracy makes backsliding more likely.

In contrast to 19th-century Western Europe, after the fall of communism in the east, there was no bourgeoisie, or capital-owning class, since capital had been owned by the state. And the groups which took over the capital after privatization, either the nomenklatura or foreign investors, did not need to resort to democratic reforms to protect themselves from a hostile political class. Foreign investors knew that the weak states of post-communist Europe couldn’t take their assets; and the nomenklatura *were* the political class—they could co-opt the state to ensure the protection of their wealth.

### Democratic Backsliding

The dependent variable of my argument is the fate of democracy in Europe from 1989 onward. In Western Europe, democracy was strong then and has remained so since, except a slight dip in some countries the last few years, particularly in Italy.[^8] But in the Eastern Bloc, democracy was nonexistent until 1989, when many formerly communist countries, such as Poland and Bulgaria, dramatically democratized. Others, such as Belarus and Russia, became slightly more democratic but subsequently reverted to authoritarianism. And some, like Ukraine and Romania, were and are somewhere in the middle in terms of democratic strength.[^9]

Of the countries which dramatically democratized in the early ’90s, some have remained solidly democratic (the Baltics, primarily, and Czechia to a lesser extent). The others have experienced what one might call “democratic weakness”: general backsliding or stretches of democracy punctuated by periods of competitive authoritarianism. Sedelmeier finds that “...a comparison of regional averages of democracy is highly suggestive of an East-West divide….\[O\]n aggregate, the eastern \[EU\] members not only continue to lag behind the western members, \[and\] the gap is widening.”[^10] This trend holds for non-EU member states as well.[^11]

While no parsimonious theory can account for all regional and temporal variation in democratic strength in post-communist Europe, my argument offers a new account of how class structure contributed to the weakness of democracy in the region. And insofar as the historical strength of the bourgeoisie instilled dormant but lasting liberal traditions in some regions during the communist period, my theory helps explain why Czechia and the Baltics have been more resistant to democratic backsliding than other post-communist states.

### The Missing Bourgeoisie

*“The most distinctive characteristic of post-communist social structure in East Central Europe is the absence of a capitalist class.” —* Eyal, Szelényi, and Townsley (1997)[^12]

A classless society (theoretically) does not allow for one social group to accumulate ownership of firms and economically pull ahead of the rest; thus, a bourgeoisie in the traditional sense cannot develop under communism. As David Ost (1993) put it, “The Leninist state prevents the formation of independent interests by nationalizing the economy, subordinating all citizens to the state.”[^13]

The obvious caveat to this is that communism did not succeed in producing a classless society: rather, a group of politically connected elites, often the managers of state-owned enterprises, “controlled” the capital. They did not receive dividends and capital gains in the way a capitalist bourgeoisie would, but they still received political and financial benefits and may have served a similar social role under communism as the bourgeoisie does under capitalism.[^14] This class, the nomenklatura, and its effect on democracy after the fall of communism are discussed later.

What is important to note here is that, because communism suppressed the natural formation of a bourgeoisie, democratization in the communist world did not come about through the channel explained in Ansell and Samuels’s “Inequality and Democratization.” Rather, we can attribute democratization in the Eastern Bloc to a range of factors, including economic dysfunction and international developments, such as the end of the Brezhnev Doctrine and EU conditionality.[^15] There was no bourgeoisie-in-waiting when communism fell, and, as the next section explains, no traditional bourgeoisie formed in post-communist Europe either.

From 1989 through the 1990s, post-communist states in Europe pursued different strategies for privatization with the goal of creating dynamic capitalist economies with private sectors and privately owned capital. Revolutionary though the transition to capitalism was, it was strongly influenced by the old communist elite, who exerted their influence on the process via the government bureaucracy, the private sector, and politics.[^16] Szablowski and Derlien (1993) characterize the transitions away from authoritarian communism to democratic capitalism as “largely the outcomes of elite structure, orientation and interaction….” And it is partly for this reason that a traditional bourgeoisie did not form in post-communist societies. Irrespective of the specific manner in which privatization occurred, it did not succeed in creating a strong and state-independent capitalist class in post-communist Europe. The three principal reasons for this are theft by the nomenklatura, foreign ownership, and weak property rights.

First, to a significant extent, the privatization of state-owned assets happened via blatant theft by the former communist party elite. They made themselves into the infamous oligarchs of eastern Europe by stealing state wealth, often via dubious legal arrangements. In the case of Poland, Maria Łoś (1992) expressed it thus: “Putting to use their many years’ experience with corruption, the black economy, and personal exploitation of the state economy, the communist nomenklatura swiftly seized the opportunities for lawful enrichment opened up by new policies and laws.”[^17] Where this happened, a new post-communist capitalist class was formed not naturally but artificially.

This group of *arriviste* capitalists was far from a traditional bourgeoisie and better described by David Ost as “the embryo of a bourgeois class-in-formation.”[^18] It was not socially separate from the state and political elite, and thus did not fear expropriation by them. It is utterly unlike the bourgeois class described by Ansell and Samuels and cannot be expected to provide sustained demand for democracy.

A key premise of Ansell and Samuels’s argument is that the bourgeoisie, because it is *socially distanced* from the political class, lacks a political voice by which to protect its interests from expropriation by the entrenched elite.[^19] Regaring the 19<sup>th</sup> century European bourgeoisie, Jürgen Kocka (1995) wrote the following: “Merchants, entrepreneurs and capitalists, professors, judges, journalists, ministers, and high-ranking civil servants differed in many respects, but they shared a sense of *social distance* from the privileged aristocracy and, on the Continent, from absolute monarchy,” \[emphasis mine\].[^20] It is the disenfranchised bourgeoisie’s push for a political voice that creates democratizing pressure. Post-communist capitalists, however, can pursue the less costly route of corruption and state co-optation to protect their wealth, since they are already integrated with the political elite.

Second, in many post-communist countries (particularly in east-central Europe), no domestic bourgeoisie formed because capital was transferred abroad, with foreign banks and other entities acquiring state-owned enterprises. For example, by 2004 in Slovakia, 79% of the electronics sector and 97% of the automotive sector were owned by foreign capitalists. The level of foreign ownership in the Hungarian manufacturing sector was 60% and in the automotive and electronics sectors it was over 90%. In 2002, the level of foreign ownership of the financial sector was 70% in Poland, 85% in Czechia, and 95% in Slovakia.[^21] Nölke and Vliegenthart (2009) write that the “political situation in \[East-central Europe\] was uniquely well suited for a full-blown institutional design geared towards the preferences of \[transnational\] corporations, given the absence in the region after 1989 of strong domestic bourgeoisies that could resist such a development.”[^22]

Instead of developing domestic capital-owning classes, these countries became dependent on foreign investors who (a) faced far less of a threat of expropriation by the state than would a domestic bourgeoisie and (b) whose interests were very different from those of such a bourgeoisie. The ruling elite of post-communist states could not realistically expropriate domestic capital that was owned by foreign investors without incurring huge costs, such as retribution by the foreign governments and banks whose assets would be stolen. This meant that these foreign investors, while they were distant and distinct from post-communist political elites, didn’t need to demand participation in politics as a means of protecting their wealth. Furthermore, foreign investors simply lack the political and cultural commitment to local democracy that a domestic bourgeoisie has. According to Nölke and Vliegenthart, “Western owners of eastern production sites may well have a certain interest in the short- and medium-term viability of their investments in \[dependent market economies\], but they have less incentive than domestic bourgeoisies to invest in the long-term sustainability of these economies.”[^23]

Third, where state-owned firms weren’t immediately expropriated by the nomenklatura or foreign investors, they often languished in informal and convoluted legal landscapes, subject to interference by the state, and controlled not by their legal “owners” but by their managers, typically the people who had been in charge of running the firms under communism. One illustrative example is that of mass privatization via vouchers, which was used in many former communist countries. This method of privatization created a “dispersed ownership structure” where stockholders lacked the “power to direct effective corporate management.”[^24] This state of limbo, in which formal ownership rights could not be asserted, created what Szelényi, Eyal, and Townsley call “capitalism without capitalists.”[^25] Diffuse and weak property rights prevented a cohesive class of capital-owners from forming. “It is impossible to identify, at the present time, individuals or groups of individuals with sufficient amounts of property who are able to exercise anything remotely similar to owners’ control of economic decision-making.”[^26]

Insofar as legal economic claims were mitigated and mediated by personal connections and political considerations, private property was still subservient to the social power structures which prevailed pre-1989 and continued afterward. According to Venelin Ganev (2009), the political economy of post-communist states is governed not by rights but by privileges, a distinction articulated by Weber in *Economy and Society* (1921). An economic right allows you to invoke state action in favour of your material interest irrespective of “questions of expediency, discretion, grace, or arbitrary pleasure;”[^27] whereas a privilege allows you to summon the coercive force of the state based on “ad hoc arrangements involving identifiable personalities,” not impersonal rules.[^28] In a polity governed by privilege rather than rights, “decisions about who gets what and how are made by a unitary bureaucracy, and … such decisions create an environment where de facto control over resources is more important than de jure orderliness….”[^29]

Weak property rights cause the value of firms to decrease as investors lose faith in their ability to control their ownership stakes.[^30] Thus, the weak property rights of post-communist Europe reduced the wealth of the post-communist capital-owning class. But it is through their wealth and relative bargaining position that the bourgeois are able to demand democratic reforms.[^31] Therefore, diffuse and weak property rights reduce both capitalists’ incentive to push for democratic protections, since they have less wealth to protect, and their ability to push for such protections.

### The Socialization of Post-Communist Capitalists

Ansell and Samuels provide an economic argument where bargaining power and material interests guide the behaviour of social classes. Their theory, when applied to politics in post-communist states, sheds light on a structural cause of democratic weakness that has been overlooked. But a brief detour into the socialization of the traditional bourgeoisie, and how it differs from that of the post-communist capitalist class, is warranted, too.

Historians, sociologists, and political scientists have emphasized that the cultural aspects of the bourgeoisie, not just its material interests, contribute to its relatively liberal and democratic orientation.[^32] The bourgeoisie is not just an economic group but a cohesive social class, with a unique culture and shared identity that sets it apart from the aristocracy, peasantry, and working class.

Kocka’s historical review of the bourgeoisie in Europe notes that a culture of thrift, self-reliance, individualism, and opposition to the state developed among the capital-owning class over many generations;[^33] it opposed the state’s economic interference on behalf of the aristocracy to uphold feudal privileges at the expense of free economic exchange. It favoured a rights-centred, rather than a privilege-centred, polity. These liberal and anti-statist values helped drive the 18<sup>th</sup> and 19<sup>th</sup> century bourgeoisie to demand democratic reforms.

In contrast to this bourgeoisie, post-communist capitalists, especially those who had been members of the nomenklatura, were socialized in a Leninist culture of state dominance and dependence. While the historical bourgeoisie associated state interference with economic hardship, to elites within the socialist state system, economic advancement came from “\[competing\] with each other only for a share of the pie distributed by the state.”[^34] The corrupt behaviour of the post-communist capitalists matches this socialization quite closely.

As I noted previously, no informative theory of social class and democracy will be able to precisely explain when and where democratic backsliding occurs. Individual national histories, contingent factors, and human agency complicate the picture and often cannot be captured by social scientific inquiry. However, it seems likely that social class structure plays a role in determining the staying power of democracy and that this is useful for understanding post-communist democratic weakness. By adding in the socio-cultural channel by which the bourgeoisie supports democracy, we can begin to account for outliers to the overall theory: the Baltic States and Czechia.

Like other East-Central European countries, they became communist only after falling under Soviet control at the end of World War II. What distinguishes them, however, is that they had developed capitalist economies with bourgeois cultural traits earlier than other post-communist countries. Bohemia, as the industrial heart of the Habsburg Empire, had developed a strong domestic bourgeoisie in the 19th century.[^35] In fact, it was the region of central Europe with the most “bourgeois” upper class.[^36] In Czech lands, the aristocracy, rather than fighting against the local bourgeoisie, had adopted its economic and cultural characteristics.[^37] And the Baltics had relatively strong urban and entrepreneurial elites (comprising mostly ethnic Germans) compared to the rest of Eastern Europe,[^38] which remained economically weak and overwhelmingly agrarian into the 20th century.[^39]

One need not merely speculate that historical legacies of bourgeois culture can account for the strength of democracy in the modern day; this has been substantiated empirically. Lankina and Libman (2021) find a correlation between the presence of the bourgeois *meschane* class in Russian districts pre-1917 and the strength of democratic competitiveness and media freedom at the district-level in post-Soviet Russia.[^40] Despite overwhelming suppression by the state, bourgeois traditions persisted via social capital, culture, and entrepreneurial values during 74 years of communism.

### Conclusion

The 18<sup>th</sup> and 19<sup>th</sup> century bourgeoisie described by Ansell and Samuels had “something to lose.” The Industrial Revolution increased their economic might and put them in contestation with an entrenched landed elite, from whom they demanded democratic protections. This dynamic, plus the liberal and anti-statist culture developed by the bourgeoisie, led to democratization in Western Europe. In the post-communist East, however, a powerful and state-independent bourgeoisie with “something to lose” did not develop, as state-owned firms were taken over by foreign investors and the nomenklatura amid a regime of weak property rights that was inimical to the development of such a bourgeoisie. Without a class constantly pushing for democratic participation as a means of protecting its material interests, post-communist states could more easily backslide into competitive authoritarianism. The lack of a strong bourgeoisie in post-communist Europe must be viewed as one among many contributing structural conditions of democratic weakness; it is not a determinative cause. Ansell and Samuels did not even intend for their theory to apply to democratic backsliding, though doing so yields interesting theoretical insights and areas for further research.
