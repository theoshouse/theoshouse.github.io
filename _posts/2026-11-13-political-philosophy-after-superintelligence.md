---
title: "Some considerations on political philosophy after superintelligence"
description: "Why I'm not convinced that handing government over to an aligned superintelligent AI would make for a good world."
---

(I'm not a hundred percent sure of these arguments, and I don't purport to be the first person to make them.)

A very simple argument can be made to justify handing over the reins of human society to a morally aligned superintelligent AI. If we grant that government is a means to an end, i.e. a way of promoting human flourishing or the moral good, and that representative, democratic government is merely the best means we've created yet, then we can argue that a vastly more intelligent being would do a better job governing, as long as it cared about human flourishing or the moral good. This argument does, however, have to hold that democracy is not intrinsically good but rather instrumentally good. Or at the very least the argument must hold that whatever the intrinsic benefits of democracy are, they can be outweighed by substantial instrumental benefits (i.e. consequences) of non-democratic good government.

The other key premiss to this argument is that an aligned superintelligent overlord would actually be a good government. One can imagine defending this by definition: "aligned" seems to mean "inclined to promote the moral good and to do so without optimizing so hard for specific, niche outcomes that it accidentally turns the world into paperclips". In AI safety we often refer to inner and outer alignment: inner deals with getting AIs to follow their goals rather than close proxies, outer deals with correctly specifying the goals we want AIs to follow (we know how to do neither right now). Say the argument is that we solve inner alignment, decide which things we care about (economic growth, egalitarianism, the environment, property rights, art, etc.), and then outer-align a superintelligent AI to all these good things -- probably way easier said than done. *Then* we hand over control of global political decisionmaking to the AI. Would it then be a good government? Would this be a good world? I'm not convinced.

Let's first recognize that this superintelligent AI overlord would make *vast* improvements to the world. Presumably, it would outlaw factory farming, create a proper carbon tax, end wars and hunger, etc. It would not be subject to the cognitive biases, follies, and silly disputes humans are saddled with. But it would call all the shots. In any situation where its preferences differed from those of people, it would win out. Democracy would be dead.

*Maybe* the aligned AI would choose to keep around liberalism and democracy, after having decided that these things are good. But then we would be stuck in the awkward position of enjoying the benefits of liberal democracy only conditionally, on the will and whim of our superintelligent overlord. Doesn't seem as good as the real thing to me.

Humans throughout history have shown a tenacious will for agency and liberty, even at the expense of the kinds of goods we can imagine a morally aligned superintelligent AI pursuing. We have higher goods than material wellbeing. Most people aspire to self-actualization and the freedom to control their destiny. Perhaps the ability to choose one path among many possibilities, with no way of knowing which is truly the "best" for one's interests, is an inherent good even when one chooses the "wrong" path. We feel satisfaction when making our own decisions and we mostly abhor the idea of a paternalistic yet benevolent authority bossing us around. One of the beauties of political liberalism to me is that it allows each to choose his own good.

Why do regional minority groups in first world countries (the Scottish, Catalan, Quebecois, etc.) pursue national self-determination, often at the expense of economic self-interest (disputed, of course) and political stability? I think that the answer (at least in part) is that most humans, upon having most of their needs met, seek higher, more abstract goods which are not easily accounted for by standard measures of well being -- goods which often depend on the fundamental freedom to choose one's path. Such goods cannot be obtained in a world where political decisions are made by a superintelligent AI.

**Potential refutations of this argument:**

> "All these people who prioritize fundamental human freedom, the ability to choose and choose wrongly, are wrong. They misunderstand their own interests. They in fact would be better off under a benevolent dictator, and thus we should create such a benevolent dictator with superintelligent AI."

Perhaps this is true, though I think anyone defending enlightened despotism ought to realize that what they're arguing is:

1. most people don't understand their own interests
2. but I do understand everyone's interests (or my AI does) -- better than they do[^kokotajlo]
3. and I've decided that all humans' interests are best served by a form of government other than democracy
{: type="a"}

This has been done before (Plato, al-Farabi, etc.), and it doesn't turn out well when put in practice (Vladimir Lenin, etc.). I think anyone tempted by this should look at the reference class for people using this argument throughout history, have a bit of humility, and reconsider their political philosophy.

> "If we can solve moral philosophy and outer align a superintelligent AI to it, then in principle it will not make bad decisions. If democracy or human agency really do matter so much, then the superintelligent AI will preserve and promote them. The superintelligent AI will have solved political philosophy!"

My response to this argument is that if you stipulate solving moral and political philosophy on your side of an argument, you're always going to win that debate; it's not particularly interesting or fruitful to argue against that which is definitionally good. Convince me that you can solve moral and political philosophy, or that an AI can solve it, and then convince me that we'll be able to confirm that the AI has solved moral and political philosophy before we hand over control of the universe to it. These seem like insurmountable problems to me, and the risks of screwing this up (infinite dictatorship) seem to outweigh the potential benefits of good governance, especially considering that we might get extremely good governance anyway one day: society has been slowly converging toward more prosperity, equality, democracy, etc. over the very long run, and I expect this to continue absent AI disaster.

[^kokotajlo]: Most giga-brain armchair interest-maximization runs into the problem that it's extremely hard, and people who do counter-intuitive things in its pursuit make predictable, bad mistakes. See [Daniel Kokotajlo](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/9RL9MuGZjzm4q3gKG/what-just-happened-a-retrospective-of-ai-alignment#5jrPYrLmpHCetrTwW) on this issue.
